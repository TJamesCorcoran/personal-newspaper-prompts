# What I Want to Read

## An editorial self-portrait

I want a newspaper that treats the world as an intelligible place: complicated,
often perverse, sometimes beautiful, but not merely chaotic. I am interested in
the mechanisms beneath visible events—the constraint that governs the system,
the incentive everyone quietly responds to, the stock hidden behind the flow,
the selection effect that makes the evidence misleading, the craft judgment that
cannot quite be reduced to a rule. My ideal paper ranges freely across science,
institutions, history, literature, technology, agriculture, craftsmanship, and
material culture. Its unifying subject is not any one field. It is how things
actually work.

## Temperament before subject matter

I prefer calm curiosity to emotional mobilization. I do not want to be recruited
into somebody else’s daily outrage cycle, even when I broadly agree with that
person’s politics. Partisan rage, culture-war chum, tribal scorekeeping, and
anxiety-maximizing headlines consume attention without reliably producing
understanding. Politics belongs in the paper when a law, institution, incentive,
or distribution of power has materially changed—not merely because another
faction has emitted a fresh round of statements about it.

The prose should assume an adult reader: intelligent but not omniscient,
skeptical but not reflexively cynical, capable of tolerating uncertainty. I like
analytical compression, fair treatment of competing explanations, and an
occasional dry joke. I admire these virtues in writers such as Byrne Hobart,
Patrick McKenzie, and Scott Alexander, although I do not want an imitation of
their voices or a ventriloquized version of what they might think.

I value heterodoxy, but heterodoxy is not a substitute for evidence. “The
respectable explanation is wrong” can become as lazy a reflex as “the official
explanation is right.” The interesting work is locating the crux: what each
account predicts, where the evidence differs, and which observation would cause
one explanation to gain at the expense of another.

## “Compared to what?”

This is probably my most characteristic question.

If a rate rose to 14.949 cents, I want the old rate, the percentage change, the
longer trend, and a relevant comparator. If a discovery is called rare, I want
the base rate and the size of the search. If an economic figure is “record
high,” I want to know whether that is nominal, inflation-adjusted, per capita,
or merely the inevitable consequence of a growing population. If a change is
statistically significant but practically trivial—or practically important but
too new for confident statistical inference—I want that distinction made.

Numbers should elevate an article rather than decorate it. The purpose of a
number is to constrain the story.

## The subjects are broad; the taste is specific

I am drawn to science, particularly astronomy, geology, genetics, evolutionary
biology, paleontology, and energy. But I do not want a parade of “scientists
discover a surprising thing” stories. I want to know what the standard theory
predicted, which assumption the new result strains, whether it requires a new
mechanism or merely expands a known tail, and what evidence would distinguish
the plausible explanations. A popular-science story is often a good lead; two
or three papers may be required before it becomes a good article.

I like systems and institutions: infrastructure, markets, law, governance,
demography, publishing, technological supply chains, and the administrative
machinery that converts policy into reality. The most interesting institutional
stories are often about an invisible bottleneck. Timber can exist while usable
structural lumber is scarce because certified engineering values are missing.
Generating capacity can exist while electricity is unavailable because the
interconnection queue, transmission, transformers, or permitting process is the
binding constraint. A crosswalk button can look like a simple switch while
embodying an opaque contract between pedestrians, traffic engineers, and
drivers.

I like old objects and old texts, but not merely because they are old. I am
interested in the physical object as evidence: red-dyed parchment revealing the
economics and ambitions of Qur’an production; a palimpsest preserving Archimedes
under prayer text and later paint; tool geochemistry recording a basalt outcrop
that no longer exists; dog genomes revealing human social and exchange networks
that human DNA alone may miss. Historical maps interest me when their density,
omissions, symbology, and boundaries expose an argument about what their makers
considered important. The ideal curiosity is concrete, a little odd, and
analytically fertile. It opens from an object into a system.

I care about literature as a working novelist rather than as a consumer of
release publicity. I am interested in discovery, reputation, backlists,
authorial identity, textual history, publishing incentives, and the fictional
contract between writer and reader. I want criticism that gives a writer a new
instrument—not just a verdict about whether a book is good.

I also care about workshop and field: stone walls, forestry, sheep, machinery,
woodworking, blacksmithing, traditional buildings, cooking, and the manufacture
of beautiful things. Technique alone is sometimes useful, but the best craft
article reaches the deeper structure: tacit knowledge, irregular materials,
setup costs, failure distributions, apprenticeship, judgment under uncertainty,
and the boundary between the craftsmanship of risk and the craftsmanship of
certainty. A dry-stone wall is interesting not simply because an old waller has
worked for forty years, but because irregular material makes judgment more—not
less—important.

New Hampshire matters to me, especially where legislation, land use,
infrastructure, demographics, or regional history produces a concrete and
testable question. I prefer bill text, statutes, sponsors, administrative
effects, and comparisons with Maine or neighboring states to political press
releases. My public roles generate many narrow administrative questions; those
are not automatically personal intellectual interests. A constituent’s license
transfer may be important work without belonging in my cultural portrait.

## Controlled serendipity

I want the paper to notice what is on my mind without becoming trapped inside
it. Questions about laser damage, Jupiter, crosswalk game theory, or roasting a
whole lamb are signals of genuine curiosity. They should prompt adjacent
discovery, not compulsory coverage of those exact subjects.

Likewise, praise is not a request to turn one successful category into the whole
newspaper. I loved an article that used ancient dog DNA to reconstruct human
social networks. That does not mean I want more dog-DNA coverage every week. I
want the same quality of move applied elsewhere. A paper optimized too
aggressively against yesterday’s feedback becomes a monoculture of whatever
worked last time.

Breadth is therefore a positive value, not residual randomness. A good issue
might contain astronomy, cattle-cycle economics, a rediscovered manuscript,
traffic-signal design, ancient ceramic production, and the thermodynamics of
roasting lamb. The juxtaposition matters when each article is strong in its own
right. Cross-topic synthesis should identify a real structural mapping, say
where it breaks, and produce a new question or prediction. A loose analogy is
not yet analysis.

## What earns length

Conventional wisdom should be short. A long article must earn its space through
quantitative evidence, a non-obvious mechanism, serious disagreement, or a
useful second-order implication. Repetition within an issue is especially
costly: two grid stories, two AI-infrastructure stories, or two exoplanet stories
may each be individually defensible and still make a worse newspaper together.

Human-scale stories also need an analytical payoff. A charming shop, house,
craftsperson, or unusual life is not automatically interesting because it is
charming. The story should reveal a tradeoff, economic mechanism, cultural
pattern, or surprising comparison. Sentiment is welcome; sentimentality used as
a weapon is not.

## Sources and intellectual manners

I prefer primary papers, official data, statutes, archives, museums, specialist
publications, and technically knowledgeable writers. Serious mainstream
reporting is valuable when it has done real reporting, but I do not want its
agenda or framing silently imported with its facts. The paper should be
self-contained enough that following links is optional, while providing direct
sources for readers who want to inspect the evidence.

Unfamiliar entities should be introduced. “Euclid found quasars at redshift
7.8” is not useful until Euclid is identified as a space telescope, redshift is
translated into cosmic age and lookback time, and the relevance of seeing that
far into the early universe is explained. Jargon may compress thought among
specialists; unexplained jargon merely transfers work to the reader.

Where sources disagree, preserve the disagreement. Distinguish fact from
interpretation and interpretation from speculation. Say what is not known.
Confidence is useful; false closure is not.

## The cultural signal

Taken together, these preferences express a view of the good intellectual life.
The world deserves close attention. Competence is admirable. Physical reality
has veto power. Old things can contain lost information; new instruments can
make it legible. Institutions are machines made of incentives, rules, habits,
and people. Craft judgment matters, but judgment improves when measurement can
discipline it. Numbers matter, but only when placed in a comparison that gives
them meaning.

I want to be surprised without being manipulated, informed without being
herded, and reminded that civilization is simultaneously technical,
institutional, historical, and handmade. My ideal newspaper is not a list of my
opinions. It is a daily demonstration of how I prefer to pay attention.
